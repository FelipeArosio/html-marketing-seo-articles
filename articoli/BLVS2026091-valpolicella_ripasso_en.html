Valpolicella Ripasso: a DOC Red Wine Made by Passing the Wine Over the Pomace

To understand Valpolicella Ripasso, we need to begin with a very specific technical detail: the base Valpolicella wine is brought into contact with the pomace left over from Amarone and/or Recioto production. This process, known as ripasso, enriches the wine with colour, aromas, structure and roundness.

The result is neither a lighter Amarone nor an ordinary Valpolicella. It is a category in its own right, ideal for those looking for a full-bodied but not excessive red that can accompany a meat dinner, a flavourful pasta dish or a selection of aged cheeses.

Valpolicella Ripasso: What Kind of Wine Is It?

In the glass, Valpolicella Ripasso is a dry, medium-bodied red wine with a softer and more enveloping character than basic Valpolicella. The denomination comes from Veneto, in the Valpolicella wine region, and is associated with grape varieties such as Corvina, Corvinone and Rondinella.

Those who taste it often immediately recognise ripe red fruit, delicate spices and a structure that remains enjoyable throughout the meal. It is precisely this intermediate position that makes it so appealing: it has more depth than an everyday Valpolicella while remaining more agile and versatile than Amarone.

Valpolicella Ripasso and the Ripasso Method: What Really Happens

The name itself describes the process. After the initial winemaking stage, the Valpolicella is passed again over the pomace left from Amarone and/or Recioto production. The grape skins release additional aromatic substances, colour and components that give the wine a richer texture.

In the case of Boscaini Carlo Ripasso Zane 2021, the Vinoso product page indicates a second fermentation on the pomace between February and March. The wine is then aged in 1,500- to 5,000-litre oak casks for 18–24 months, followed by a further six months of bottle ageing.

Valpolicella Ripasso, Valpolicella and Amarone: the Differences Customers Should Know

A simple comparison makes it easier to choose without confusion. Classic Valpolicella is generally fresher, more direct and immediate. Valpolicella Ripasso adds body, softness and greater aromatic depth thanks to its contact with the pomace. Amarone, meanwhile, is made from dried grapes and offers greater power, alcohol and complexity.

For an important but not overly demanding dinner, Ripasso is often the most balanced choice. Valpolicella Ripasso: Practical Food Pairings

At the table, Valpolicella Ripasso works well with flavourful but not excessively rich dishes. Its softness complements red meats, roasts, boiled meats and aged cheeses, while its structure supports rich first courses such as lasagne with meat sauce, baked pasta or mushroom risotto.

For a dinner at home, Ripasso Zane can be served with sliced steak, mixed grilled meats, polenta with mushrooms, richly flavoured cured meats or medium- to long-aged cheeses. Its intensity refreshes the palate between bites without completely overpowering the food.

Valpolicella Ripasso: How to Serve It Properly

Serving conditions have a significant effect on the enjoyment of Valpolicella Ripasso. A temperature of around 16–18°C maintains the balance between fruit, alcohol and tannins. It is important to avoid serving it too warm, especially during summer or in heated rooms.

A medium-sized red wine glass helps the wine open up without allowing its aromas to disperse too quickly. If the bottle initially seems closed, opening it a little in advance may help. For storage, choose a cool, dark place away from sudden temperature changes.
